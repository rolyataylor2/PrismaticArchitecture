# Emotional Formation of Parts

Part formation is strongly connected to emotional impact. Emotionally significant experiences are more likely to produce persistent specialization because emotional impact signals: “This matters.” It does not necessarily signal that something is bad.

Salience may involve fear, pain, attachment, love, loss, betrayal, shame, excitement, wonder, success, failure, helplessness, responsibility, belonging, rejection, novelty, surprise, relief, or repeated frustration. Positive experiences may form Parts as readily as negative ones.

Formation is interpretive. The simulation does not assign psychological significance; the Agent determines whether an experience matters enough to alter Parts or contribute to formation. Formation may reflect emotional magnitude, repetition, novelty, existing Parts, Identity, prediction error, unresolved conflict, developmental state, and perceived future importance. A relational recognition Part can be useful without high emotional charge; charge is not a required Part type or a proxy for activation Score.

Sustained mismatch between desired Identity and available behavior may become emotionally significant: repeated incongruity can produce frustration, failure, or conflict and contribute to introspective or corrective Part formation.

Emotional impact does not automatically create a new Part. It may instead activate or adapt an existing Part, specialize, split, change relationships, or justify genuinely new formation. Avoid uncontrolled proliferation.

## Emotion is inferred, not a required taxonomy

OntoAgentic does not require a hand-authored taxonomy, fixed emotion label, or emotional vector for Parts to function. Emotional meaning may be inferred from beliefs, memory, relational context, mission, expectations, consequences, and current investment. Experimental emotional representations remain optional.

## Full emotional range

Shame, anger, jealousy, fear, pride, attachment, grief, embarrassment, excitement, relief, affection, guilt, curiosity, frustration, longing, and other human affective perspectives remain valid cognitive information. They may inform safety, belonging, performance, attachment, competence, loss, boundaries, opportunity, and responsibility. Unpleasant or socially negative affect must not be excluded architecturally.

Formation should consider inferred significance rather than matching named categories. Strong positive investment can create Parts just as negative experience can.
